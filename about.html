I turn ideas into interfaces, interfaces into motion, and motion into meaning.

The story

I started the way a lot of creative developers do: taking things apart to see how they worked, then rebuilding them slightly stranger than before. Somewhere between my first canvas experiment and my first shader, I realized the browser wasn't just a document viewer—it was an instrument.

Since then, my work has lived at the intersection of design and engineering. I build fluid simulations that respond to a cursor like water responds to a hand. I give typography mass, velocity, and the ability to shatter. I synthesize sound from movement so interfaces don't just look alive—they sound alive.

I work independently by choice. It keeps me close to every decision, from the first strategic question to the final easing curve.

How I work

Every project starts with a question worth answering, not a deliverable worth invoicing. I prototype early and often, because the prototype is the strategy: a working sketch reveals more in an afternoon than a deck reveals in a month.

I care about the details people feel rather than see—the damping on a spring, the dissipation of a fluid field, the two milliseconds of attack on a gain envelope. Those decisions are where an interface stops feeling authored and starts feeling inevitable.

Clarity is not the absence of complexity. It is complexity, resolved.

Beyond the screen

When I'm not shipping interfaces, I'm usually in Blender sculpting procedural materials, writing field notes about the math behind my projects, or chasing whatever rabbit hole currently has my attention. Curiosity is the only metric I track obsessively.

If you have an idea with a pulse—something that deserves more than a template—I'd like to hear about it.
